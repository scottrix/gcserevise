H1: Kaiser Wilhelm and Germany

Foundation Higher AQAEdexcelOCREduqasCCEA

Vesalius, Harvey and Paré; the printing press and humanist challenge to Galen; Sydenham's bedside observation; the Royal Society; continuity in treatment and the Great Plague of 1665.

📋 Key Concepts: The Medical Renaissance (1500–1700)

The printing press (c1440s, reached England 1476) changed everything: identical, accurate copies of texts (including Galen in the original Greek) spread fast — and so did challenge to Galen, because scholars could now compare translations and spot errors. The Reformation weakened the Church’s grip on learning (monasteries dissolved 1536–39, their hospitals gone). Humanism — "back to the originals" — inspired anatomists to check Galen against human bodies.

IndividualWhat they didWhy it mattered
Andreas VesaliusDe Humani Corporis Fabrica (1543); dissected humans himself; corrected ~300 Galen errorsProved Galen only dissected animals — licence to question
William HarveyDe Motu Cordis (1628): blood CIRCULATES, pumped by the heartBroke Galen’s blood-flow model; method: observation, dissection, maths
Ambroise ParéLigatures instead of cauterising wounds; ointments for gunshotPractical surgery progress from battlefield experience
Thomas SydenhamObserved patients directly; distinguished diseases; "English Hippocrates"Revived bedside observation over book theory

Great Plague 1665: responses still looked medieval (quarantine, red crosses on doors, killing cats and dogs — which made it worse, prayer, herbs against miasma) but with new elements: plague doctors, searchers of the dead keeping bills of mortality, government quarantine orders. Change at the edges, continuity at the core.

📝 Continuity: Why Most Treatment Did Not Change

Vesalius and Harvey changed KNOWLEDGE, not treatment: there was still no germ theory, no effective drugs, no anaesthetics or antiseptics. Physicians still bled and purged; apothecaries still sold herbs; the four humours lingered in practice into the 18th century. Harvey’s own contemporaries attacked him (his old teacher ridiculed him); training lagged behind discovery by decades. Meetings and institutions seeded the future: the Royal Society (1660) published and debated new science (Philosophical Transactions, 1665) — a channel that would later carry Jenner, Pasteur and Koch.

Examiner’s pointer: the best answers treat the Renaissance as a change in methods and authority (observation over texts; printing; royal societies) rather than a change in care. Say that explicitly.

🧮 Worked Examples — Exam Style

“The printing press was the most important reason for medical progress in the Renaissance.” How far do you agree? (16 + 4 marks)

Plan — P1 printing: identical texts, Galen’s errors visible, Vesalius’s Fabrica illustrated and copied across Europe. P2 individuals: Vesalius/Harvey made the discoveries — printing only spread them. P3 attitudes: humanism gave scholars the CONFIDENCE to test authority. P4 institutions: the Royal Society created a permanent arena. Judgement: printing was the accelerator — but humanist attitudes and individuals were the engine; without Vesalius there is nothing to print.

Four-paragraph structure, each anchored to a FACTOR, with a weighed judgement — the 16-mark formula on every thematic paper.

❓ Practice Questions

Q1: Name Vesalius’s 1543 book and its key claim about Galen.

Q2: Describe two features of William Harvey’s work on circulation.

Q3: Give two ways Paré improved battlefield surgery.

Q4: Describe two features of responses to the Great Plague of 1665.

Q5: Explain why the Reformation affected medicine in England (two ways, 4 marks).

Q6: "Renaissance doctors cared more about challenging Galen than about treating patients." How far do you agree? (16 + 4 marks)

✅ Answers

  1. De Humani Corporis Fabrica (1543) — dissected humans himself and exposed hundreds of Galen’s anatomical errors (e.g. the jawbone, the uterus).
  2. Proved blood circulates (valves, one-way flow); demonstrated the heart works as a pump via dissection and calculation — published in De Motu Cordis (1628) using observation and experiment over textual authority.
  3. Ligatures to tie arteries instead of burning flesh with hot oil/cautery; soothing ointments for gunshot wounds instead of the accepted (brutal) treatment.
  4. Quarantine and shuttering of infected households (red crosses, 40-day isolation); searchers of the dead producing weekly bills of mortality; (also: killing dogs/cats, prayers, miasma-defending herbs).
  5. Monasteries dissolved (1536–39) — hospitals and care networks lost; Church authority over learning weakened, so humanist challenge to Galen became safer. (Also: university curricula freed up; printing spread Protestant-backed new learning.)
  6. Plan — YES: Vesalius/Harvey targeted texts, not bedsides; humours still used in treatment; Sydenham the exception. NO: Paré = bedside surgery; Sydenham’s clinical observation; Harvey’s work eventually reoriented training. Judgement: challenge led care by decades — the statement is half-true for the elite, wrong for the bedside.

📌 Key Facts to Memorise

  • Printing (c1440s; England 1476) — accurate texts + spread of challenge
  • Vesalius, Fabrica 1543 — human dissection, corrected Galen
  • Harvey, De Motu Cordis 1628 — circulation, experiment-based proof
  • Paré — ligatures, ointments (battlefield, 1530s–60s)
  • Sydenham — bedside observation, disease distinction ("English Hippocrates")
  • Royal Society 1660; Philosophical Transactions 1665
  • Great Plague 1665 — quarantine + bills of mortality; humours/miasma still core beliefs
  • Reformation 1536–39 — hospitals lost, authority loosened

🗺️ In the Exam

The Renaissance rewards paired comparisons: knowledge changed (Vesalius, Harvey) while treatment stayed humoral; authority shifted (text → observation) while the Church loosened its grip. In "how far" questions, mark the SHIFT explicitly — methods and attitudes changed faster than care. For interpretation questions on the Great Plague, contrast 1665 with 1348: same beliefs, slightly more organised government — continuity IS the interpretation point.

⚠️ Common Errors

  • Wrong: "Vesalius discovered circulation" Correct: Vesalius = anatomy corrections; Harvey = circulation (1628)
  • Wrong: "the Royal Society invented germ theory" Correct: the Royal Society was a publishing/debating forum (1660) — germ theory came in the 1860s–70s
  • Wrong: "Paré used antiseptic" Correct: Paré used ligatures and ointments — antisepsis is Lister, 1867
  • Wrong: "1665 plague responses were scientific" Correct: quarantine was organisationally new, but prayer and miasma-avoidance dominated

🧭 Board Focus — Eduqas

Routes A and B. Both routes combine a period study, depth studies and a thematic study with non-British elements. Eduqas papers use structured (part-labelled) questions — answer every lettered part, as later parts build on earlier ones.

Always check: specification details change — confirm paper structure and set texts against the current specification for your exam year.

🎯 Exam Tips

  • Pair every Renaissance individual with a FACTOR: Vesalius = technology (printing) + attitudes (humanism); Paré = war; Harvey = method (experiment)
  • Always separate KNOWLEDGE progress from TREATMENT progress — the paper rewards the distinction
  • 1665 vs 1348 comparisons are high-yield: organisation changed, beliefs did not
  • Quote book titles with dates: Fabrica (1543), De Motu Cordis (1628)
