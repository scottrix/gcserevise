H1: Kaiser Wilhelm and Germany

Foundation Higher AQAEdexcelOCREduqasCCEA

Hippocrates, Galen and the four humours; miasma and supernatural beliefs; physicians, apothecaries and barber-surgeons; the Black Death of 1348–49; progress and continuity in medieval medicine.

📋 Key Concepts: Medicine in the Middle Ages

Between c1250 and 1500 medicine rested on two pillars: Hippocrates (clinical observation, the four humours) and Galen (whose theory of opposites and animal dissection dominated for 1,300 years). The Christian Church preserved Galen’s writings and enforced his authority; miasma (bad air) explained most disease; supernatural explanations coexisted with natural ones.

BeliefWhat it meantTypical treatment
Four humoursIllness = imbalance of blood, phlegm, black bile, yellow bileBleeding, purging, opposites
MiasmaBad air caused diseaseSweetsmelling herbs, avoiding swamps
SupernaturalSin, planets, witchcraftPrayer, pilgrimage, charms
Galen’s oppositesTreat hot with coldCooling foods for fevers

Who treated you depended on wealth: physicians (university-trained, expensive, urine charts), apothecaries (herbal remedies), barber-surgeons (bleeding, amputation, no anatomy training), wise women and the household itself. Hospitals were religious institutions — care, not cure; most were destroyed at the Reformation or dissolved under Henry VIII.

Case study — the Black Death (1348–49): killed perhaps a third of England. Explanations mixed supernatural (God’s punishment, planetary alignment in 1345), miasma (corrupt air from earthquakes) and contagion-by-sight. Treatments: prayer, confession, carrying herbs, avoiding breathing bad air — quarantine attempts appeared in Italian ports. Continuity outweighed change: responses to plague in 1348 look like responses to later outbreaks in 1563 and 1665.

📝 Progress and Its Limits (c1250–1500)

Real progress: medical training improved (Renaissance-style dissection began late in the period: human dissection was legally permitted from the 14th century in a few universities); Islamic medicine — Ibn Sina (Avicenna)’s Canon of Medicine and Al-Razi’s work — entered Europe via translated texts and raised clinical standards; John of Arderne (surgeon, anal fistula operation, c1350) showed careful technique and professional pride; medical regimens (the Regimen Sanitatis) promoted diet, exercise and moderation — Hippocratic prevention for the wealthy.

Why change was slow: the Church promoted Galen because his ideas fit Christian teaching (the body as God’s design); dissection was limited; printing had not yet arrived, so books were copied by hand and error-ridden; war (the Hundred Years’ War) and plague cycles drained resources; physicians had status invested in NOT challenging Galen.

Examiner’s pointer: questions rarely ask "was medieval medicine bad?" — they ask you to weigh progress against continuity with SPECIFIC evidence on both sides, and to explain why (factors: Church, individuals, science and technology, war, attitudes).

🧮 Worked Examples — Exam Style

Describe two features of medical treatment in the Middle Ages (4 marks).

Model: « Treatment was dominated by the theory of the four humours: physicians rebalanced the body by bleeding or purging patients. Second, supernatural explanations mattered: patients might go on pilgrimage or pray to saints because illness was often seen as God’s punishment. »

Two distinct features, each with a specific detail and one sentence of development — that is the full 4 marks. Vague answers ("treatment was bad") score nothing.

Interpretation question (8 marks): "Medieval hospitals were more about care than cure." How far does the source support this?

Method: (1) detail what the source shows (wards, religious staff, prayers for the soul); (2) link each detail to care vs cure; (3) add context the source CANNOT show — hospitals were founded as religious houses, physician treatment was separate, leprosy institutions isolated the sick; (4) judgement: the source supports it but is incomplete. Never spend the marks re-describing the source alone.

❓ Practice Questions

Q1: Name the two ancient authorities who dominated medieval medicine.

Q2: Describe two ways the Christian Church shaped medicine c1250–1500.

Q3: Give two treatments a medieval apothecary might sell and the belief behind them.

Q4: Describe two features of the Black Death of 1348–49.

Q5: Explain why new medical ideas spread slowly in the Middle Ages (two reasons, 4 marks).

Q6: “Medieval medicine owed more to religion than to science.” How far do you agree? (16 + 4 marks)

✅ Answers

  1. Hippocrates (four humours, clinical observation) and Galen (opposites theory, authority on anatomy).
  2. The Church preserved and taught Galen in university curricula, and founded/run hospitals as religious care institutions; it also discouraged dissection and challenges to Galen.
  3. Herbal laxatives to purge excess humours; sweet-smelling posies/w herbs against miasma. (Any two with the belief named.)
  4. Killed roughly a third of England’s population; explained by God’s punishment/planets/miasma; responses included prayer, confession, quarantine in some ports, herbs against bad air.
  5. Church control of learning defended Galen; no printing — hand-copied texts spread slowly and inaccurately. (Also accepted: limited dissection, war/plague disruption, physician status invested in Galen.)
  6. Essay plan — YES side: Galen preserved BY the Church; hospitals = prayers and care; plague responses religious. NO side: regimens and Arderne = empirical care; Islamic texts entered via translation (science); urine charts and observation = Hippocratic method alive. Judgement: religion DOMINATED delivery and explanation, but a genuine scientific strand persisted — answers that hold both sides and reach a reasoned verdict hit the top band.

📌 Key Facts to Memorise

  • Four humours (blood, phlegm, black bile, yellow bile) — Hippocrates; opposites — Galen
  • Miasma theory; supernatural explanations coexisted with natural ones
  • Practitioners: physician / apothecary / barber-surgeon / wise woman / home remedies
  • Hospitals: religious, care-not-cure, ~470 by 1500 in England, lost at the Reformation
  • Black Death 1348–49: ~⅓ dead; explanations supernatural + miasma + contagion
  • Progress: Ibn Sina’s Canon, John of Arderne c1350, Regimen Sanitatis, early legal dissection
  • Continuity reasons: Church authority, no printing, limited dissection, social order

🗺️ In the Exam

In the thematic papers this period appears as the START of the narrative — examiners test whether you can characterise it precisely: continuity built on Galen + Church + supernatural beliefs, WITH pockets of progress. Compare-forward questions ("how similar was treatment in 1500 to 1700?") need BOTH periods’ specifics: humours still ruled in 1500, but by 1700 printing, Vesalius and the collapse of Galen’s authority had changed the ground rules. Name the factor for every point of change or continuity: the factor IS the explanation.

⚠️ Common Errors

  • Wrong: "people were ignorant and used only prayer" Correct: natural (humours, miasma) AND supernatural explanations coexisted — both could be used for the same illness
  • Wrong: "Galen was forgotten until the Renaissance" Correct: Galen was taught and used throughout — the question is WHO challenged him, and when
  • Wrong: "hospitals cured the sick" Correct: care, shelter and prayer; cure was not their function
  • Wrong: "the Church stopped all medicine" Correct: the Church FUNDED most medicine — it shaped and preserved it, while also limiting challenges to Galen

🧭 Board Focus — OCR

Spec J410 — two routes: History A (Explaining the Modern World) and History B (Schools History Project). Your route decides the topics; check which route your centre takes. OCR's depth-study questions reward precise narrative chains — cause, event, consequence in order.

Always check: specification details change — confirm paper structure and set texts against the current specification for your exam year.

🎯 Exam Tips

  • Anchor every paragraph with a factor word: Church, individuals, science/technology, war, government, attitudes
  • Use dates sparingly but exactly: 1348 (Black Death), c1250 start, 1500 end
  • For interpretation questions: evidence from the source + context BEYOND the source + a judgement
  • Learn 2 progress examples AND 2 continuity examples — every "how far" question needs both
