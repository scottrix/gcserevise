H1: Kaiser Wilhelm and Germany

Foundation Higher AQAEdexcelOCREduqasCCEA

The succession crisis after Edward the Confessor, four claimants, the battles of Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings, and William's coronation — with Hastings tactics and the Bayeux Tapestry debate.

📋 Key Concepts: Claim, Conquest and Control (1064–87)

The succession crisis: Edward the Confessor died childless (January 1066) with four claimants: William of Normandy (alleged 1051 promise + Godwinson oath of 1064), Harold Godwinson (chosen by the Witan, crowned immediately), Edgar Aetheling (blood claim, aged ~14), Harald Hardrada (Tostig’s alliance plus a deal with the Pope helped William gain support).

BattleDateOpponentOutcome
Gate Fulford20 Sep 1066Tostig + HardradaHarold’s earls Edwin & Morcar defeated
Stamford Bridge25 Sep 1066Hardrada & TostigBoth killed; Viking threat ended
Hastings14 Oct 1066WilliamHarold killed (arrow/blade to the eye per Bayeux Tapestry); William crowned Christmas Day 1066

Establishing control 1066–87: motte-and-bailey castles (3,000+, built in days), the Harrying of the North (1069–70) after the 1069 rising (Yorkshire devastated — Domesday records it "waste"), Submission of the earls (1075), and replacing the Anglo-Saxon aristocracy: by 1086 only two surviving major landholders were English.

📝 Feudalism, Landholding and Domesday

Feudalism: the king owned all land; tenants-in-chief (barons/bishops, ~170) held it for knight service; knight’s fees (~4,000–5,000 knights owed ~40 days’ military service); peasants at the bottom. Sanctuary and salvage: introducing forests (royal hunting, harsh forest law) and the murdrum fine (a hundred paid 46 marks if a dead Norman was found with no killer produced — protecting the conquerors).

Domesday Book (1086): the great survey — who owned what, what it was worth under Edward and now; purposes: taxation, dispute settlement, control. "No Briton ever described England so thoroughly" — it legalised the conquest’s land grab by RECORDING it.

📝 Life Under the Normans: Villages, Towns, Church

Village life: most were villeins (tied to the lord’s land, working ~2–3 days/week on demesne land); life revolved around the manor, harvest cycles and the village priest. Towns: growth of trade; Norman castles replaced Anglo-Saxon burhs; Jews invited over as moneylenders (Christians could not legally lend at interest).

Church: William replaced English bishops/abbots with Normans (by 1087 only Wulfstan of Worcester survived among senior figures); Lanfranc as Archbishop of Canterbury (1070) rebuilt discipline, raised cathedral schools, extended the hierarchy; monasticism flourished; Norman Romanesque architecture — massive cathedrals (Durham begun 1093) and castles as statements of power.

William Rufus (1087–1100) and Henry I (1100–35): succession struggles, Odo’s rebellion (1088), Malcolm III’s raids, Robert Curthose’s Normandy rivalry — Henry won Normandy at Tinchebray (1106); the Ango-Norman realm stabilised; Henry’s Coronation Charter (1100) promised to end Rufus’s abuses.

🧮 Worked Examples — Exam Style

"The Harrying of the North was the main reason Norman control succeeded." How far do you agree? (AQA 16 + 4)

YES: the 1069–70 harrying destroyed the North’s ability to resist (Domesday shows "waste"; chroniclers record famine, even cannibalism reports); no northern rising after 1070. NO: castles garrisoned across the country were the daily instrument of control; land confiscation removed resistance leadership; Stamford Bridge had already destroyed the alternative dynasty. JUDGEMENT: the harrying finished the north specifically; castles + landholding held everything — argue "harrying was the bluntest, castles the constant, instrument".

Describe two features of the feudal system (4).

« All land was held from the king in exchange for military service — about 170 tenants-in-chief subinfeudated to ~4,000–5,000 knights. Second, obligations ran downward too: lords owed protection and land, vassals loyalty and service, binding society in a pyramid. »

❓ Practice Questions

Q1: Describe two features of the Battle of Hastings.

Q2: Give two reasons William built motte-and-bailey castles so rapidly.

Q3: Describe two features of the Harrying of the North.

Q4: What was the murdrum fine, and what does it reveal?

Q5: Explain why William commissioned the Domesday survey (two reasons, 4 marks).

Q6: "Castles were the most important way the Normans established control." How far do you agree? (16 + 4 marks)

✅ Answers

  1. Harold fought exhausted after Stamford Bridge and a forced march south; the shield wall held until the Norman feigned-flight retreats drew out and killed Harold (and his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine).
  2. Speed — earth-and-timber construction in days with forced labour; dominance — a small garrison could hold a district and overawe rebellion.
  3. 1069–70 devastation of Yorkshire/Durham (crops, livestock, villages burned — Domesday records 60%+ "waste" in some wapentakes); famine the following winter; northern resistance to the conquest effectively ended.
  4. A fine on a hundred when a Norman was found dead without his killer produced — shows Normans FEARED for their safety and legalised collective responsibility to protect them.
  5. Taxation — know exactly what could be levied; legal control — settle land-disputes between Normans and fix ownership permanently.
  6. Plan: castles (daily military dominance), landholding (a new loyal elite), harrying (devastating the north), the Church (legitimacy + Lanfranc). Judgement: castles WERE the most visible instrument, but land-transference made control permanent — weigh temporary vs permanent mechanisms.

📌 Key Facts to Memorise

  • 1064 oath · Gate Fulford 20/9 · Stamford Bridge 25/9 · Hastings 14/10 · crowned 25/12/1066
  • Harrying 1069–70 · Submission of the Earls 1075 · Revolt of the Earls 1075
  • Feudal pyramid: king → ~170 tenants-in-chief → ~4,000–5,000 knights → peasants
  • Domesday Book 1086 (taxation + disputes) · Lanfranc Archbishop 1070 · murdrum fine 46 marks
  • Rufus 1087–1100; Henry I 1100–35; Tinchebray 1106

🗺️ In the Exam

This option is British-depth + historic environment (AQA: castle-focused). For the historic-environment question, know ONE castle in detail — e.g. Warkworth or Pleshey or Pevensey: building phases, defensive features (motte height, bailey functions, tower keeps), how design responded to the threat level. For interpretation questions the Bayeux Tapestry and Domesday entries are the default sources — practise "how useful" with purpose (propaganda/tax record) and missing context.

⚠️ Common Errors

  • Wrong: "Harold died from an arrow to the eye — proven fact" Correct: the Bayeux Tapestry shows a figure with an arrow; other accounts say Harold was cut down by knights — say "sources disagree"
  • Wrong: "the Harrying was in 1066" Correct: 1069–70, three years into the reign, after the northern rising
  • Wrong: "Domesday recorded people" Correct: land, resources and VALUES — a tax audit, not a census of persons

🎯 Exam Tips

  • Control narrative = castles + landholding + harrying + Church + law — deploy as factor paragraphs in that order
  • Hastings tactics: shield wall vs cavalry/archers; the feigned retreat ×2 — tactical detail earns the description marks
  • For 16-markers, always name the factor in the topic sentence, evidence second, judgement weighed against a rival factor
