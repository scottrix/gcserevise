R4: Structure Analysis

Foundation Higher AQAEdexcelOCREduqasCCEA AO2

Analysing how writers organise and structure their texts for effect.

📋 What is Structure Analysis?

Definition: Structure analysis examines how a text is organised - the order of ideas, paragraphing, sentence variety, and narrative techniques used to shape meaning.

Structure analysis involves:

  • Examining how the text begins, develops and ends
  • Analysing paragraph organisation and length
  • Looking at sentence variety and length
  • Considering shifts in focus, time or perspective
Remember:
Language = WHAT words are used
Structure = HOW the text is organised

📝 Key Structural Features

Whole Text Structure

Openings: How does the text begin? What effect does the opening have?
- In media res (starting in the middle of the action)
- Setting the scene
- Creating mystery or intrigue
Endings: How does the text conclude? What is the final impression?
- Resolution or closure
- Cliffhanger or ambiguity
- Reflective circular ending
Narrative Perspective: Who is telling the story?
- First person (I, we) - personal, subjective
- Third person (he, she, they) - more detached
- Omniscient narrator - knows everything

Paragraph Level

  • Paragraph length: Short paragraphs for impact, long for detail
  • Topic sentences: Opening sentences that introduce the main idea
  • Focus shifts: Changes in subject, time, place or perspective
  • Repetition: Ideas or phrases that recur throughout

Sentence Level

Sentence Length Effects

Short sentences: Create tension, drama, urgency

"She stopped. Listened. Nothing."

Long sentences: Build detail, create flow, slow pace

Sentence Types

Simple: One clause - clear, direct, emphatic

Compound: Two or more main clauses - balanced, connected

Complex: Main + subordinate clause - layered, detailed

🔍 How to Analyse Structure

Questions to Ask

  • Where does the writer's attention focus at the beginning? How does this change?
  • Are there shifts in time, place, or perspective?
  • How does paragraph length affect pacing?
  • What information is revealed and when?
  • How does the ending connect to the beginning?

Structural Analysis Framework

Beginning: How does the text start? What does the writer focus on?
Middle: How does it develop? What shifts occur?
End: How does it conclude? What's the final effect?
Example Analysis

The text begins with a close focus on the narrator's immediate surroundings, creating claustrophobia. The writer then zooms out to the wider landscape, suggesting freedom beyond. This structural shift mirrors the narrator's emotional journey from trapped to liberated.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake Correction
Analysing language instead of structure Focus on organisation, not word choice
Listing structural features without effect Always explain the effect of structural choices
Ignoring the whole text structure Consider beginning, middle and end
Forgetting sentence-level analysis Comment on sentence variety and length
Not using structural terminology Use terms like "focus", "shift", "perspective"

📊 Mark Scheme Guidance

AQA Paper 1 Question 3 (8 marks)

  • Level 4 (7-8 marks): Detailed analysis of structural features with clear effects
  • Level 3 (5-6 marks): Clear analysis with some explanation
  • Level 2 (3-4 marks): Some understanding of structure
  • Level 1 (1-2 marks): Simple or irrelevant comment
Examiner's Tip: This question often causes confusion with language analysis. Focus on WHERE things are placed and WHY - not what words mean. Think about the "journey" through the text.

❓ Practice Questions

Q1: What is the difference between language and structure analysis?

Q2: Why might a writer use a short paragraph?

Q3: What does "in media res" mean?

Q4: How can sentence length affect the pacing of a text?

✅ Answers

  1. Language analysis looks at word choice and literary devices; structure analysis looks at how the text is organised and sequenced.
  2. To create impact, drama, tension, or to draw attention to a key moment or idea.
  3. Starting in the middle of the action - a narrative technique that creates immediate engagement.
  4. Short sentences speed up pacing and create tension; long sentences slow pacing and allow for detailed description.

🎯 Exam Tips

  • Trace the reader's journey through the text
  • Comment on beginnings, middles and endings
  • Look for shifts in focus, time, or perspective
  • Use structural terminology: focus, shift, perspective, sequence
  • Consider paragraph and sentence length
  • Don't analyse language - that's a different question!

📝 Exam Technique

Structure Analysis Exam Strategy: This is AQA Paper 1 Q3 (8 marks) - spend 10 minutes. The key is to think about the journey through the text: where does the writer's focus start, how does it shift, and where does it end? Avoid analysing word choices - that's Q2. Instead, comment on paragraph lengths, sentence variety, shifts in focus, time, or perspective, and how the text moves from beginning to end. Examiners want to see you trace the structure, not just spot isolated features.

Time management: Q3 = 10 minutes for 8 marks. Write 2-3 paragraphs tracing the structure from beginning to end.

What examiners look for: Analysis of structural choices (not language), clear explanation of effects, use of structural terminology (focus, shift, perspective, sequence), and an understanding of how the whole text is organised to create meaning.

⚠️ Common Errors

Watch Out!

Students often analyse language when the question asks for structure. Wrong: "The word 'dark' creates a spooky atmosphere." Correct: "The writer places the description of the dark room at the opening to immediately establish a claustrophobic focus, before shifting to the wider landscape."

Students often list structural features without explaining their effect. Wrong: "The writer uses short sentences and long paragraphs." Correct: "The single short sentence 'She stopped.' interrupts the flowing description, creating a sudden halt that mirrors the character's realisation."

Students often ignore the overall shape of the text and focus on isolated moments. Wrong: Making separate points about random paragraphs. Correct: Tracing how the text moves from a close-up on the character's fear to a wider view of the landscape, showing how this structural shift reflects her growing sense of freedom.

✍️ Model Answer

Full-Mark Response

8 marks: How has the writer structured the text to interest you as a reader? You should write about what the writer focuses your attention on at the beginning, how this changes, and how it ends.

The writer structures the extract as a journey from confinement to liberation, mirroring the narrator's emotional trajectory. The text opens with an intensely close focus on the narrator's immediate surroundings: the "narrow cell" of her bedroom, with its "cracked ceiling" and "sealed window." This spatial restriction, placed at the beginning, traps the reader alongside the narrator, sharing her claustrophobia. The middle of the extract marks a pivotal structural shift: a single short paragraph - "She opened the door." - breaks the dense description, creating a moment of dramatic pause that signals change. From this point, the focus widens progressively: first the hallway, then the garden, and finally the "open sky" that concludes the extract. This structural movement from confined interior to boundless exterior mirrors the narrator's emotional release, and the reader experiences the same expanding sense of possibility. The cyclical echo of the colour blue - from the "bluebottle" trapped against the window at the start to the "limitless blue" of the sky at the end - structurally reinforces this journey from entrapment to freedom.

Mark scheme: Level 4 (7-8 marks) requires detailed analysis of structural features with clear effects. Must trace the whole text from beginning to end, using structural terminology, and explain how structure shapes the reader's experience.

📊 AO Deep Dive

Assessment Objective Analysis

AO2 for structure requires you to analyse "how writers use... structure to achieve effects and influence readers." This is fundamentally different from language analysis - you must focus on where things are placed, how the text moves, and why the writer organised it this way. At grade 5, students identify some structural features but revert to language analysis, or make structural points without explaining effects. At grade 9, students trace the reader's journey through the text, analyse how shifts in focus or perspective create meaning, and explore the relationship between the beginning and end. The key is thinking structurally: not "what does this word mean?" but "why has the writer placed this here, at this point in the text?"
